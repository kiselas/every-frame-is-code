# Silent social video: the genres and their rules

Most of a feed is watched with the sound off. Instagram, Facebook and LinkedIn autoplay muted; surveys put muted viewing at 69% in public places and about 85% of all plays where mute is the default, and captions raise the share of people who watch to the end by about 80%. A video for Reels, Shorts or TikTok is therefore a **silent film with music as a bonus layer**: everything the viewer must know is on screen, and the sound only helps the fifth who turned it on. This chapter is the rulebook for that film and a map of the genres it comes in. The frame, the safe zones and the type are in [19-vertical.md](19-vertical.md); how to make the pictures carry an argument is in [21-infographics.md](21-infographics.md).

## The silent contract

The viewer holds a phone at arm's length, with the thumb on the screen, in a feed of other people's videos, with the sound off, and decides within two seconds whether to stay. The contract that follows:

1. **The picture is the film.** Take the audio away and nothing must be lost. Music sets the mood and marks the beats for those who hear it; it never carries a fact.
2. **No narrator means no "as you can see".** Without a voice, text is the only sentence the film speaks. It states the claim; the picture proves it; the caption gives the source. Text that describes the picture ("a diagram of the method") and a picture that repeats the text (the word GROWTH next to an arrow going up) are each saying nothing.
3. **Every frame is a poster.** People scrub and pause. Any frame taken at random must make sense on its own: a readable statement, a picture that belongs to it, the context (the year, the chapter) in the HUD. This is also the thumbnail test: the platform picks a frame, and it must sell the film.
4. **Reading is the clock.** The viewer reads at up to 3 words per second (160-180 words per minute is the broadcast caption standard; faster is for people who already know the words). A statement stays at least 2 s, and the whole film holds about 2.5 words per second of running time: **a 50-second film has a budget of about 120-150 words**, statements and captions together. Cut words before cutting pictures.
5. **The first 1.5 seconds are the whole audition.** Reels ads that keep their audience land the hook inside 1.5 s; TikTok's working rule is 3 s, and 70% of viewers still watching at 3 s is the line below which a video is not pushed. Frame 0 already moves, the question is asked in words by second 2, and nothing is explained before it is asked.
6. **Completion is the metric.** Watch time and completion weigh about half of TikTok's ranking. Benchmarks: ~70% at 3 s, ~60% at 15 s, ~50% at 30 s. The practical consequences: 30-60 s, no middle sag (the complication goes in the middle), an ending that resolves in the last 2 s and visually returns to the first frame, so a replay feels like a loop.
7. **One idea per frame, one claim per picture.** The eye has one band of attention on a phone; a frame with two pictures, or a picture and a list, is read as neither.
8. **The source is on screen.** A number with no source is an opinion in a bold font. The caption line under the statement is where the source lives; it costs no reading time because nobody has to read it.

## The silent grammar

Every shot is three layers, each with one job:

| Layer | Job | Form | Budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statement | the claim, one idea | up to 3 lines, at most 16 condensed capitals a line, one payoff word in the accent color | 2-5 s, at most 8 words |
| Picture | the proof: a quantity, a mechanism, a comparison, an object | one encoding, one accent, builds in reading order, holds its final state | the whole shot |
| Caption | the source, the unit, the arithmetic | one monospace line | read by those who want it |

Plus the persistent layer, the HUD, which carries the through-line (a year, a count, a metric) so that a paused frame has its context.

Grammar rules:

- **Setup, then payoff, on the beat.** The statement's small words come first; the payoff word lands last, larger, in the accent color, on a strong beat, and the picture's decisive move (the bar reaches its value, the stamp lands, the count stops) happens on the same beat. Payoff in text and payoff in picture are one event.
- **The picture starts before the text ends.** By the time the payoff word appears, the picture is already drawing, so the eye has somewhere to go.
- **Anaphora carries rhythm without sound.** Repeated openings ("1952. ...", "1955. ...", "1959. ...") let the viewer predict the form and read only the new word. Music does this with a motif for the hearing; the text must do it alone.
- **Transitions mean something.** Three to five types per film, each with one meaning: a cut for the next step, a swipe up for the next item of a list, a dip for a new chapter, a flash for the reveal, a punch for the twist. A silent viewer reads the cut as grammar; random cuts are noise.
- **Motion is a sentence.** A silent viewer reads movement as "something changed". Move what means something (the data, the object), keep still what does not (axes, frames, titles). Decorative particles are noise in a language the viewer is trying to read.

## Universal rules

Numbered, to be cited in reviews.

1. Frame 0 moves; the hook question is on screen by 2 s; no title card, no logo, no fade from black.
2. The word budget is 2.5 words per second of film; a statement is at most 8 words and stays at least 2 s; a line is at most 16 condensed capitals.
3. The payoff word and the picture's decisive move land on the same beat.
4. Every number has a yardstick on screen (a comparison, a scale, a before) and a source in the caption.
5. One picture per frame; one claim per picture; one accent color for the datum.
6. Nothing a viewer needs sits in the top 10% or the bottom 22%; the right 11% is free of text (the button column).
7. Any random frame passes the poster test: statement + picture + context, readable at 540 px wide.
8. Text never overlaps the picture; the picture never repeats the text; neither describes the other.
9. The ending calls back to the first frame and resolves in the last 2 s; the last statement reframes the thesis.
10. Audio is a bonus layer: the film is reviewed once with the sound off before it is reviewed with it.
11. A held shot still moves (push-in, sway, a live element), but nothing moves without a reason.
12. The script is checked as text first: write the statements and captions in a list, count the words, read them at three per second. Only then draw.

## The genres

Formats that need a person on camera (talking head, skit, reaction, vlog), a screen recording or found footage are outside this kit. What a code-drawn film can be:

| Genre | The promise to the viewer | Spine | The picture is... | Length | Text budget | Kills it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **How it works** | "You will understand X" | question, mechanism in 3-5 steps, complication, fix, the everyday image understood | a mechanism that runs: a token on a path, parts that move, a signal that travels | 30-60 s | 100-150 words | steps shown as labeled boxes; a step without its "why" |
| **One life** (biography) | "This person did X, and here is how" | label or myth, the person, the idea, the fight, the legacy, the label reconsidered | a timeline as the HUD; one proof-picture per deed; props (a page, a stamp, a document) | 40-60 s | 120-160 words | a list of dates and titles; names a viewer does not know as the picture |
| **One number** (data story) | "This number will surprise you" | the number, where it comes from, what changes it, what it means for you | a counter with a yardstick, a bar against a reference, a unit chart | 20-45 s | 60-100 words | the number alone, with no scale; a second number before the first has landed |
| **Timeline** (history in a minute) | "How we got here, fast" | eras in order, one idea each, accelerating | a rail with a marker; one picture per era that transforms into the next | 45-60 s | 120-160 words | equal time per era; slides instead of a continuous world |
| **Versus / before-after** | "A against B" | the two side by side, one criterion at a time, the verdict | a split frame, the same encoding on both sides, synchronized | 20-40 s | 60-100 words | different scales left and right; more than 4 criteria |
| **Myth vs fact** | "What you believe is wrong" | the myth told straight, the crack, the evidence, the real story, the myth restated and corrected | the myth's own picture, then the same picture corrected | 30-50 s | 90-130 words | announcing it is a myth in the first line (no crack to feel) |
| **Countdown / list** | "N things, best last" | N items in reverse order of weight, each a mini-shot | a numbered rail; one picture per item in the same grammar | 30-60 s | 10-14 words per item | items of unequal size; no reason for the order |
| **Process / how to** | "Do X in N steps" | steps in order, each shown done | the thing being made, growing step by step; the step number in the HUD | 20-45 s | 8-12 words per step | explaining instead of showing; a step that skips |
| **Statement** (kinetic typography) | "Read this" | one text, built word by word, with one turn | the text itself: size, weight, color and motion are the picture | 10-25 s | 20-50 words | more than one typeface idea; motion that fights the reading |
| **Teaser** | "There is a longer thing" | 3-5 peaks from the long film, a question, where to find it | the long film's best frames, re-composed vertically | 10-20 s | 15-30 words | telling the ending; no question |
| **Loop** (ambient) | "Watch this turn" | a formula of time that returns to its start | a demoscene effect, a mechanism, a pattern | 5-15 s, seamless | 0-6 words | a visible seam; text that must be read |

### Requirement cards

**How it works.** The hook is the everyday image with a question on it (a map and a dot: "how does it know?"). The mechanism is shown by *running* it: a token moves through the steps, a signal propagates, a part turns. Each step is a statement with the step's own picture, never a labeled box. The complication is a visible failure of the mechanism (the dot drifts, the loop never exits). The fix changes one visible thing. The last shot is the hook's image, now understood. Reference: [examples/gps/](examples/gps/) (landscape, with sound) and [14-long-form.md](14-long-form.md).

**One life.** Open on the tension, not the birth: the label, the misconception, the thing that was impossible. The person enters second, by name, with two or three facts that set them up for the deed. Each deed is one shot with one proof-picture: *what the idea looked like*, *what it replaced*, *how much*. Never a list of posts and titles. The through-line is a timeline in the HUD with the year marker; the motif is one object that changes meaning (a stamp, a page, a tool). Names of students and colleagues are not a picture: show *how many* and *what they did*, or do not show them. Close on the opening image, transformed. Reference: [examples/lyapunov/](examples/lyapunov/) and its critique in [21-infographics.md](21-infographics.md).

**One number.** The number is the hook *and* the ending: it appears early, large, with a question, and comes back at the end with its meaning. It needs a yardstick in the same frame (a known quantity, a before value, a human scale) before it means anything. Everything in the middle is where the number comes from (its arithmetic, as counters) and what moves it (one variable at a time). Encode with length or position, never area; animate the bar, not the axis.

**Timeline.** A rail with evenly placed marks is a lie about time; use a true scale, or a log scale labeled as such. Eras get screen time by *importance*, not duration; the pace accelerates toward the present. One picture per era, and the eras share a world: the same object, tool or map transformed, not a new slide. The marker in the HUD never stops moving.

**Versus / before-after.** Split the picture band in two; both halves use the same encoding at the same scale and change at the same time. One criterion per shot; the statement names the criterion, the halves show it. The verdict is a visible merge or a visible winner, not a line of text. Before/after is also the strongest *shot-level* structure in any genre: when a claim is "X replaced Y", show Y and X side by side.

**Myth vs fact.** The myth is told sincerely for 5-8 s with its own picture, so the viewer feels the crack when it comes. The crack is a visible contradiction in the same picture (a bar that does not reach, a date that is wrong). The evidence is one picture with a source. The ending restates the myth with one word changed.

**Countdown / list.** A visible counter in the HUD (5... 4... 3...). Items have equal length (2 bars each) and the same shot grammar, so the viewer learns the form by item two and watches for the content. Number one gets double time and a different transition. Say in the hook why the order is what it is.

**Process / how to.** Show the thing being made, in the frame, growing; the step number in the HUD; the statement is the instruction in the imperative, at most 6 words. A step that cannot be shown is a step that should be cut or merged. End on the finished thing, whole, held for 2 s.

**Statement.** Only when the text is the point (a quote, a thesis, a manifesto). One typeface in two weights, one accent color, one turn (a word that changes meaning, a line that reverses). Rhythm comes from word size and timing; read [08-kinetic-typography.md](08-kinetic-typography.md). Never animate letters in a way that slows reading below 3 words per second.

**Teaser.** Cut from the long film's peaks, re-composed for 9:16 (each shot re-framed, not cropped). Ask the long film's question, show three answers in motion, do not show the ending, close on where to find it (the HUD's title is enough).

**Loop.** No text to read, or a single line that stays. The last frame equals the first (`draw(t)` with a period: `t mod T`). The seam is the only review question; render two periods and watch the join.

## Choosing the genre

The material chooses:

| You have | Genre |
|---|---|
| a surprising quantity | one number |
| a mechanism people use without understanding | how it works |
| a person and a deed | one life |
| a common belief that is wrong | myth vs fact |
| two things people confuse or compare | versus |
| a sequence of eras | timeline |
| a set of items with an order | countdown |
| a sequence of actions | process |
| a text worth reading twice | statement |
| a longer film | teaser |
| a pattern that is beautiful in motion | loop |

If the material fits two genres, take the one whose picture is stronger: a biography with one great number can be a one-number film with a person in it.

## Checklist

- [ ] The sound is off for the first review and the film still makes complete sense
- [ ] Frame 0 moves; the question is on screen by 2 s
- [ ] Word count at most 2.5 times the seconds; no statement over 8 words; none shorter than 2 s on screen
- [ ] Each payoff word lands with the picture's decisive move, on a beat
- [ ] Each number has a yardstick in the frame and a source in the caption
- [ ] No picture is a list of labels in shapes ([21-infographics.md](21-infographics.md), the label test)
- [ ] Three random paused frames pass the poster test
- [ ] Transitions: 3-5 types, each with one meaning, used consistently
- [ ] The ending returns to the first frame and resolves in the last 2 s
- [ ] The genre is named in `SCRIPT.md`, and its requirement card was checked against the shot table

## Pitfalls

- **Writing for a narrator.** A script that reads well aloud has twice the words a silent viewer can take. Cut every connective; the picture is the connective.
- **Facts as pictures.** "Student of X", "Order of Y", a list of names: these are captions, not pictures. A frame whose picture is text in boxes has no picture.
- **Two numbers in one frame.** The second one erases the first. One number, then the next shot.
- **Explaining the picture in the statement.** The statement is the claim ("THE MACHINE KNEW ONLY NUMBERS"); the picture is the proof (a column of octal). "HERE IS MACHINE CODE" is a caption pretending to be a statement.
- **The equal-time timeline.** Twelve eras at 4 s each is a slideshow. Importance decides time; the pace accelerates.
- **A hook that is a title.** "ALEXEY LYAPUNOV, 1911-1973" is a tombstone. "IN 1954 THIS WORD WAS A VERDICT" is a question.
- **Music as the structure.** A drop that nobody hears is not a chapter boundary. Mark boundaries in the picture: a theme change, a transition type, a HUD change.
- **Trusting the viewer to know the names.** They do not. Show what the name did, or show how many there were.
